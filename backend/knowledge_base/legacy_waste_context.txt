Central Pollution Control Board (CPCB), Ministry of Environment, Forest and

NOTE: This document is primarily about municipal-scale landfill/dumpsite
remediation (bio-mining and bio-remediation), not individual household
waste disposal. It's included here mainly for background definitions and
context on why proper segregation at the household level matters - poor
segregation upstream is a major cause of the legacy waste problem this
document addresses.

--- WHY HOUSEHOLD SEGREGATION MATTERS (context) ---

Historically, mixed waste dumped without segregation accumulates into
"legacy waste" - massive uncontrolled dumps (some over 50 metres high in
Indian cities) that generate methane (a potent greenhouse gas), leachate
that pollutes groundwater, and frequent fires. Segregating waste properly
at the source (household level) is what prevents this from continuing to
happen with fresh waste.

--- RELEVANT DEFINITIONS ---

Segregation: the unmixed, separate storage and management of different
solid waste components - biodegradable waste, non-biodegradable waste
(including recyclable waste), non-recyclable combustible waste, sanitary
waste, non-recyclable inert waste, domestic hazardous waste, and
construction/demolition waste.

Sorting: separating recyclable categories such as paper, plastic,
cardboard, metal, glass, etc. from mixed waste to enable recycling.

Extended Producer Responsibility (EPR): the responsibility of a
producer/brand owner for environmentally sound management of a product
(including its packaging - plastic, tin, glass, cardboard) until the end
of its life.

--- WHAT HAPPENS TO RECYCLABLES DURING WASTE PROCESSING (context) ---

When mixed/legacy waste is processed, recyclable materials recovered
include: plastics, metals (ferrous and non-ferrous), glass, paper,
cardboard, and textiles/rags. Ferrous and non-ferrous metals recovered
from waste screening are sent for recycling. Heavy fractions including
glass and construction debris are typically crushed and reused on-site
(e.g., in road sub-grade) or sent for recycling where possible.

This confirms metal and glass are both established recyclable categories,
even though this document doesn't give household-level disposal
instructions for them specifically.
